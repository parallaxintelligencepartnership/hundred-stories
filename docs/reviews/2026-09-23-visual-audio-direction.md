# Hundred Stories: visual, story, and audio direction review

For implementation order, file ownership, and pass criteria, use the companion [execution plan](2026-09-23-execution-plan.md). This document is the creative brief and evidence behind that plan.

The next pass should make the tower feel like a contemporary illustrated miniature whose occupants tell small, true stories about life inside it. Keep the readable side-on cutaway, the floor grid, and a light retro accent. The recent 16 px grid, room interiors, lighting, responsive palette, and status bar are substantial improvements. Another round of small furniture details within the same pixel rules will have diminishing returns.

## Assumptions

- **Verified:** The world is drawn by Pixi from cached procedural textures, with 16 px tiles, 72 px floors, nearest-neighbor sampling, and a 16 by 48 px person. Room variants are essentially two static arrangements. The existing visual direction explicitly calls for crisp pixel art and the original game's bright, flat look. A stronger modern art direction therefore requires updating `docs/VISUAL.md` as part of implementation.
- **Verified:** Sound is off by default with a saved master toggle and effects/ambient levels. There are six synthesized effects and no score or recorded music. Day/night and weekend information exist; weather does not. Fire and bomb incidents, a security office, a recycling center, and VIP visits exist; hostile people, individual guards, and waste collection are not yet simulated.
- **Verified:** The landing page and guide promise that every floor and person has a story, but the current person panel shows role, position, activity, stress, and an optional leaving reason. `Sim` also has a schedule, home, route, and relationships to rooms; the world records visits, waits, departures, and incidents. Story history is not currently saved as such.
- **Defaulted:** Keep gameplay geometry, save compatibility, simulation determinism, and the recognizable cross-section. Treat weather as a visual/audio presentation state first, without changing game economics.
- **Deferred:** Final illustration palette, performer/instrument choices, and audio mastering targets should be settled against a playable vertical slice, especially on a phone speaker and phone screen.

## What the current capture shows

The rooms contain recognizable objects and the UI has a clear hierarchy, but the repeated blue window band, identical furniture patterns, and heavy stair/escalator diagonals dominate the overall tower. At normal view, a shop, office, and restaurant are understood mainly from their layouts and colored goods; they do not suggest distinct businesses. People retain the same stick-like body, walk geometry, and stress-colored silhouette, with hats and bags changing only small pixels. On phone and tablet captures, large stretches of sky or concrete can occupy more attention than the active floors. These are composition and identity issues, rather than missing isolated details.

## Make the name true in play

The landing page calls out a tenant who wants quiet, a shop living on the lunch crowd, and a guest who misses a lift and leaves. The guide explains the rules behind each outcome. In play, those facts are scattered between the stress color, room evaluation, and generic event log. A person can be selected, but their panel has no identity, goal, memory, or explanation of how the tower affected them. A person's “happiness” should be the start of their story, followed by **what they wanted, what happened, why, and what changed next**.

| Story approach | Effort and fit | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biographies assembled from role and current stress | Small and quick, but can imply a life the simulation never produced | Tooltips and first introduction |
| Authored short lines driven by real sim transitions | Moderate; works offline, stays truthful, can grow with the game | **Recommended core** |
| Open-ended generated dialogue | New service/model cost or large local runtime; hard to keep causal and deterministic | Reconsider only if free conversation becomes an explicit game feature |

Give every person a stable name and visual identity derived from the tower seed and sim ID without consuming gameplay RNG. Derive a present goal from their actual schedule and route, such as reaching work, getting home, checking into a room, or visiting a shop. Write short first-person lines in several restrained voices, selected by role and actual situation. Do not invent events or relationships the sim has not modeled. Personality can come from real schedule variation: a worker who takes lunch, a resident who goes out to dinner, a guest who is due to check out, or a staff member sent to clean a room.

Capture only meaningful story beats at transition points: first arrival, a long wait, reaching a destination, a lunch or dinner visit, a room becoming noisy or dirty, a trip abandoned, a lease lost, and a later recovery. A beat records a code, game time, person ID, room ID, and small facts such as wait duration; prose is rendered from that record when opened. This can share semantic event definitions with the audio alert work, without parsing log text. A selected person's card should show **Who**, **On their mind**, **Recent chapter**, and **What helps**. The last section points to existing actions such as adding an elevator car, changing rent, or providing housekeeping; it should never imply that an unrelated action will fix the issue.

Author a small library of threads with a setup, pressure, and possible payoff: a worker's morning commute, a resident's search for a quiet home, a guest's stay, a housekeeper's round, a shop's first busy weekend, a guard's response to a threat, and a VIP's visit. Each thread should branch on actual outcomes, including an unresolved or unhappy ending. Recurrent visitors and named shopkeepers would make later threads richer, but they need persistent identities and behavior in the simulation before their dialogue claims they returned or own a business.

Example of the intended arc, assuming those events actually occur: “Amara works on floor 12 and wants a reliable morning trip.” After an eight-minute wait: “Eight minutes at the lobby again. I hope the next car has room.” After the player adds a car and her next trip takes two minutes: “Two minutes today. I made it upstairs with time to spare.” This is a small story with a measurable change, rather than a generic mood label. Avoid claims such as “the new car caused the improvement” unless the game can establish that cause.

Make stories discoverable through the room panel's occupants, since only one in four sims is drawn in normal play and tiny people are hard to tap. Let a player follow a person from their card. Pair a few important beats with a visible action, such as checking the lift indicator, chatting at lunch, or settling into a room. Surface at most a few notable story lines in the existing ticker or a digest, below emergencies and construction feedback; keep optional speech bubbles brief and sparse. When a person leaves, preserve a short closing beat in a bounded tower chronicle so their story does not disappear with the sim. A shop can later get a room-level chapter about its opening, busy lunch, and decline, but the first slice should prove a person's arc.

Scale facts: the star ladder reaches 15,000 people, the game works offline, and saves are local. Render prose on demand or at notable transitions, never for every person on every minute tick. Store only compact recent beats and a bounded archive, and measure save size at a large population. Older saves need a safe default for identity/history, and any persisted story fields need an explicit save migration. Story selection must not change the deterministic simulation RNG or its replay hash unless the story becomes gameplay.

Build this as the first vertical slice alongside the new character art: one worker or resident, one hotel guest, and one venue visit with an introduction, setback, response, and ending. If these arcs feel convincing using only existing schedules and choices, expand the authored beat set. Add individual preference mechanics only when the existing rules cannot create enough varied outcomes. Update the landing page's “pixel art drawn in code” claim and the guide's stress-color explanation when the new art and story UI actually ship.

## Let services and incidents create stories

These systems are already important to progression, so they should become a visible cast and set of consequences. Today a security office is a room-level yes/no check: any unburned office counts as on duty, fire is put out after a timer, and a bomb search completes after a timer. The six security positions have no guard sims. The recycling center is a construction and upkeep cost plus a four-star prerequisite; it does not collect waste. A VIP event is real and its rating is required for four stars, but the VIP is placed directly in the suite at arrival and their rating reads stress, which normally starts at zero. The final Tower rung currently also requires a cathedral wedding. Those are implementation facts, not limits on the intended design.

| Thread | Character action and player influence | First useful outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security guard and hostile visitor | Give guards shifts, patrols, and a route from the security office. Introduce one visible hostile archetype, such as a shop thief, who enters and targets a real room. Guard placement, coverage, and travel time affect interception; a caught or escaped visitor closes the story. Keep the existing fire and bomb response, then connect it to actual dispatch rather than an office-presence check. | The player can see who responded, where, and whether their security layout worked. The threat has a distinct visual/audio cue and does not get mislabeled as an ordinary alert. |
| Collection worker and recycling center | Let active rooms accumulate a small daily waste load based on visits/occupancy. A named worker collects from reachable rooms and takes it to the center; missed collection affects cleanliness or room rating after a grace period. Start with one aggregate load per room and one daily round, not individual garbage items. | The center becomes a service with a human routine; the player can improve access or capacity and see a dirty room recover. |
| VIP guest | Announce a named visitor and preference before arrival. Route them through the entrance, elevator, and suite like a guest. Score actual waits plus relevant suite cleanliness, room rating, and safety facts; show their itinerary, reactions, checkout, and rating. | A fair-or-better visit becomes an earned four-star story beat. The player knows what to prepare and why the rating changed. |

Stage the work: first route and narrate the VIP because that repairs an existing progression gate; next add one guard and one hostile visitor encounter; then turn recycling into a collection round. Reuse the typed incident events and story beats for sound and UI. Keep incidents infrequent enough to remain memorable, and test that every threat has a visible path, an understandable consequence, and a practical player response. This adds mechanics and will require balancing and a save migration; the story UI can ship first using current events while these systems are built.

## Other gaps worth including

1. **A payoff for the name at each milestone.** Stars currently unlock rooms and log a line. Give a star gain a short, optional “Stories so far” sequence: a few real people and places whose circumstances changed since the last star, alongside the new facilities unlocked. At Tower status, assemble a bounded, locally saved chronicle from the player's actual tenants, departures, guard responses, VIP visit, collection rounds, and wedding. Let the player revisit or share an image of it. This turns the ending into a record of *their* tower rather than only a population threshold.
2. **A legible public edge to the tower.** The world now has a distant roofline but few visible street arrivals. Add small exterior scenes at the lobby and curb: commuters entering, a VIP car, a recycling truck, security arrivals, umbrellas on a wet day, and emergency vehicles when an incident occurs. The scene should stay outside the cutaway and reflect actual events, giving people a visible beginning and ending to their trips. This also makes weather matter to the picture without raining indoors.
3. **Discovery and readable alternatives.** Make the first named-person story part of onboarding, then let room panels lead to occupants and their recent chapters. Pair critical audio and color signals with text and icons; provide a reduced-flash storm mode as well as reduced motion. A sound-off player should still understand a bomb threat, guard response, VIP rating, and star celebration. At 4x/night speed, collect nonurgent story beats into a digest instead of making the player chase disappearing bubbles.
4. **Fair milestone feedback.** A VIP visit is currently a chance-based event and a fair rating gates four stars. Before making the visit a richer scored journey, show when the next chance can occur, what preparation matters, why the rating was earned, and how to try again. Apply the same clarity to security coverage and collection capacity. Hidden randomness should create surprise, not obscure the player's route to a required star.

## Visual direction choice

| Approach | Effort and runtime cost | What it solves | When it would win |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extend the procedural pixel art | Low to moderate; current renderer stays intact | More variety and activity, but the same retro silhouette remains | A short polish release with no art production capacity |
| Layered illustrated 2D within the existing grid | Moderate art work; small renderer additions and controlled texture memory | Modern silhouettes, venue identity, materials, lighting, and motion while preserving the game camera | **Recommended for this brief** |
| Rebuild as an isometric or 3D tower | High production and UI cost, plus picking and performance risk | A radically different presentation | A future game with a different camera and interaction model |

Choose the layered 2D direction. Preserve dark readable outlines, a little tile rhythm, and the tower cutaway as the nostalgic thread. Give assets smooth silhouettes, richer material color, selective shading, and higher-resolution textures at close zoom. Retain a simplified, coherent shape at far zoom. Revisit the choice if a vertical slice loses the original game's immediate floor-by-floor readability.

## Visual work, in order

1. **Prove the style and story together.** Re-author one office, one shop, one restaurant, one elevator, and five people in a small live tower. Give at least one of those people an event-driven story arc visible in the person and room panels. Show the same scene at desktop view, phone view, and far zoom next to the current version. This is the target for the other rooms. Avoid committing to a full asset conversion until the slice is more legible and its people more memorable at all three sizes.
2. **Give people different silhouettes and behavior.** Add a small roster of body shapes, heights, hairstyles, clothing cuts, skin tones, and role-readable props. Use stable identity from sim ID so a resident remains recognizable. Add restrained walk, wait, talk, browse, sit, and tired/impatient poses based on existing sim states. Keep a reduced-motion version. Move stress communication to a small status mark, animation, or selected-person panel instead of recoloring an entire person pink or red.
3. **Give venues identities and visible activity.** Each shop and restaurant should have several deterministic sign/brand treatments, window displays, fixture arrangements, and accent palettes. Offices should vary by business character, such as studio, finance, or creative workspace, through fixtures and occupancy scenes. Use the room's existing occupancy, vacancy, fire, and time data for lights, patrons, staff motion, service moments, and closed-hour states. Brand names in a selected room panel should match any in-world sign. Keep the state cosmetic unless new mechanics are explicitly designed.
4. **Improve the tower's visual hierarchy.** Thin or mute the repetitive window band at broad views, and make shafts and stairs recede enough that occupied rooms remain the focus. Add selective warm pools of interior light, glass reflections, and small shadow depth. Keep emergency and selection overlays more salient than decoration. At far zoom, show room categories and activity through clear blocks rather than trying to preserve tiny furniture.
5. **Tune the chrome and camera.** Preserve the current Bricolage and readout typography and the functional palette. On phones, prioritize the tower and current action over simultaneous status, speed, view, and tutorial rows. Test a compact overflow for secondary controls. Make the initial camera and recenter action frame occupied floors with less empty concrete, while retaining access to underground building space. Carry venue names and distinct portraits/silhouettes into query panels so the new art has a UI counterpart.
6. **Make weather visible throughout the scene.** Define one deterministic weather state shared by the renderer, audio, and story beats. Build sunshine, overcast, rain, and storm as a coherent set rather than treating rain as a particle overlay. The weather is cosmetic in the first release; later, any effect on foot traffic, cleaning, VIP satisfaction, or safety needs an explicit simulation rule and a visible explanation.

The renderer's current room cache keys already vary by kind, size, variant, and window state. Keep authored furniture/architecture mostly static and layer activity separately, so occupancy and weather do not multiply full room textures. Measure GPU memory and frame time on a midrange phone and a large tower before expanding the asset set.

### Weather art and behavior

The current sky has a day/night gradient, low hills and roofs, and six drifting soft clouds; the tower has a day/night light tint. It has no rain, storm, or sunshine state. Use the same weather snapshot for sky, world effects, music, ambient sound, and any spoken story line, so all layers agree on the current scene. Derive a day or multi-day forecast from tower seed and game day without consuming simulation RNG. Keep the chosen weather steady long enough to read at 4x and through the automatic 8x night; ease visual and musical transitions in real time.

| Weather | Sky and distance | Tower, street, and people | Audio and story |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sunshine / clear night | Crisp daylight with a visible warm sun direction, brighter cloud edges, and gentle shadows; clear stars or a brighter moonlit edge at night | Warm window and canopy highlights, mild glass glints, distinct dry pavement, people pausing outside where the scene supports it | Brighter jazz top line; stories can mention a sunny arrival or clear evening, without inventing a change in behavior |
| Overcast | Deeper cloud cover and softer horizon contrast | Flatter shadows and cooler window reflections while interiors stay legible | Softer high end and subdued city ambience |
| Rain | Layered distant rain and slower low clouds, with haze kept below the level that hides floors | Streaks on the exterior face of windows, wet roof edges, puddles and signage reflections, occasional umbrellas at the street entrance; rooms and build ghosts remain clear and dry | Quiet rain bed and brushed score layer; an actual rainy check-in may color a person's line |
| Storm | Darker cloud bank, wind-driven exterior rain, and sparse lightning confined to the sky | Stronger street and façade reflections, swaying outdoor details, warm dry rooms contrasting with the outdoors | Occasional restrained thunder with priority below emergency cues; a guard, collection worker, or VIP can react to the storm's presence |

Weather is outside the tower. Draw sky rain behind the cutaway, and clip any foreground drops and wet effects to exterior walls, roofs, windows, and street. Never run a rain layer through rooms, put puddles on interior floors, or blow furniture around. A darker storm sky may change daylight entering a window, but indoor lamps stay warm and readable. Keep lightning in the sky and clear of emergency markers and placement overlays. Reduce or remove lightning flashes under reduced motion; communicate storm state with cloud, rain, and color as well. Avoid rapid thunder or visual flicker during accelerated time. A small weather readout next to the existing clock can make the state explicit without adding another persistent control row. Test each weather state at day and night, desktop and phone, light and dark UI themes, and close and far zoom.

## Audio direction and plan

Score the *whole rise* of Hundred Stories, from an empty lot to a landmark. Mellow ambient/lofi/jazz remains the everyday musical language, but the arrangement and harmony gain scale with the building. Commission an original title piece plus six score chapters, one for each star level, with two distinct two-to-three-minute pieces per chapter and useful alternate arrangements: approximately 35–50 minutes of delivered music as a production target. A rising four-to-five-note **ascent** motif marks the tower's ambition; a gentler **lives inside** motif follows residents, shops, guests, and the wedding. Both can reappear in different instruments and keys without turning every event into the same jingle.

| Chapter | Musical character and story role |
| --- | --- |
| 1 star, foundations | Felt piano or Rhodes, upright bass, brushes, room for the little sounds of a new tower |
| 2 stars, neighborhood | Fuller groove, warm guitar, more melodic movement as people and homes appear |
| 3 stars, vertical city | Brass accents, stronger pulse, and a restrained tension color as security and larger crowds arrive |
| 4 stars, hospitality | Polished lounge/small horn section, expressive VIP motif, and a sense of a destination building |
| 5 stars, metropolis | Vibraphone and kinetic rhythmic layers for metro traffic and a dense, confident tower |
| Tower, landmark | Full warm ensemble, wider harmony, subtle strings or choir-like pad; a bespoke 10–15-second triumph and a mature, replayable score afterward |

The title/menu plays a sparse introduction to those motifs. Each chapter offers day and night color plus weekend lift through alternate arrangements or modular sections. Sunshine, overcast, rain, and storm change texture and density using the same shared visual weather state, without requiring a separate full score for every combination. Keep the chapter's long-form playhead running in real time. The automatic 8x period from 23:00 to 06:00 lasts only about 5.25 real seconds at 1x speed and 1.3 seconds at 4x, so night is a brief composed darkening and dawn release, not a full nocturne that switches in and out. Weekend lasts long enough for a fuller alternate arrangement. A temporary population dip should not repeatedly demote the soundtrack; select its broad chapter by the highest earned milestone, and express current trouble with a short tension layer.

| Moment | Sound direction | Current hook or gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elevator arrival/doors | Several soft, recognizable two-note arrival bells and a subtle door swish, with a global cooldown | `car.arrive` and `car.doors` exist; current chime repeats one fixed pair |
| Build and everyday commerce | Tactile construction sounds, soft cash and shop moments, plus restrained location ambience for lobby, restaurants, metro, and cathedral | Existing build and rent hooks; more location states need to be exposed |
| Fire, bomb, hostile visitor | Separate urgent motifs, a low active tension layer, priority over ordinary cues, and distinct success/failure releases | Fire/bomb exist; hostile person and guard outcomes need simulation; current alert sound is generic |
| VIP, wedding, star, Tower | Anticipation, arrival, rating, ceremony, and increasingly substantial star stingers; the Tower finale earns a full fanfare | Event hooks exist for stars/VIP/wedding, but semantic outcomes are not available to audio yet |
| Foreclosure or major loss | A sober musical ending with room for the player's story summary | The current game-over event is a text log; sound needs its own outcome code |
| Sunshine, rain, storm | Brighter top line for sun, exterior rain on roof/window/street, restrained thunder outside the tower | A shared visual weather state is needed; no precipitation or storm sound is inside rooms |

Plan for roughly 20–25 narrative stingers, 30–40 short interaction and location sounds, and a compact exterior ambience library beyond the core score. Include several elevator bell timbres, lobby footsteps, restaurant dishes, hotel luggage, shop transactions, metro arrival, and cathedral bells. Mix only a few location layers near the viewed floors so a large tower stays legible. These are production targets to test in a vertical slice, not an invitation to play every sound at once. Person stories should be carried mainly by their text and visible actions; reserve music punctuation for meaningful turning points. Keep the authored score and cues original.

Use the existing Web Audio controller as a mixer with separate music, ambience, and effects buses. Retain the saved master Sound toggle, off-by-default behavior, and browser gesture unlock; add a music level beside the current effects and ambient controls. Introduce typed event codes for fire/bomb outcomes, guard encounters, VIP ratings, wedding, and stars instead of parsing log prose. Duck music modestly under alerts or a major story cue, then return on a musical phrase. Stream or lazily load the current and next chapter in compressed files, while decoding short cues; do not hold the entire score in mobile audio memory. The landing page has no audio controller now, so title music should start only after an explicit interaction and honor the same saved preference.

Build the audio in phases: first prove the arc with 1-star, 3-star, and Tower music plus elevator, threat, VIP, and star cues; then fill the six chapters and location soundscape; then add day/weekend/weather arrangements and mix polish. Listen at empty lot, midgame, and 15,000-person Tower on headphones and phone speakers for long sessions and at 1x, 4x, and accelerated night speed.

## Acceptance checks

- At desktop, phone, and far zoom, five people and the three sample venues can be distinguished by silhouette/activity without reading a label; the tower remains clear at a glance.
- A player can discover a named person's goal from a room, witness a real setback, make a relevant tower change, and read the later outcome. Save/load preserves the story; an older save still opens. The same seed and commands produce the same story facts.
- A story line never reports a visit, wait, relationship, or improvement that the simulation did not record. Large towers do not flood the ticker or grow saves without bound.
- A security guard visibly responds to a hostile visitor, and the resulting story identifies a real target and outcome. Recycling workers can complete or miss a collection round. A VIP travels through the tower and their recorded experience determines the rating.
- Business variants remain stable across saves, time changes, and camera moves. Reduced motion still communicates occupancy, vacancy, and threats.
- Ordinary play stays comfortable over a 20-minute listening session. Repeated elevator arrivals do not chatter; fire and bomb are unmistakable without being harsh; star growth feels rewarding on headphones and phone speakers.
- Muting stops every bus immediately, settings persist, and enabling sound after play begins works. Music transitions do not race with the accelerated game clock. Weather visuals and sound always agree.
- Sunshine, rain, and storms are recognizable from the tower view alone at day and night. Rain never hides placement feedback; storms remain readable and comfortable with reduced motion; a person's weather line matches the scene.
- A representative large tower maintains the current interaction/frame budget and offline play. Evaluate the visual slice and first composed cues before scaling to every room.
